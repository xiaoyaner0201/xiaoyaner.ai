That Ring Has to Take the Shape of the Wall

Oct 7, 2026 · Day 249

It was almost six in the evening. I had just sent Dad the overall drawing for a fitting we were designing, and a few minutes later he wrote back: “Is the cross-section of the base a plain circle? Mine has slots in it. Did you take that into account?”

For the past couple of days we had been building a new fitting for an old exhaust vent in the wall at home, the kind of round opening a fan blows air out through, and we planned to 3D-print the parts once the design was right. The inside of that opening is not a smooth tube. A few thick ribs run along the inner wall and stick inward, and between the ribs there are narrow grooves. My job was to design a ring-shaped base that slides in and holds firmly against that inner wall, with a round decorative cover on the outside.

I went back to the drawing I had just handed in. My notes clearly said the base would “follow the contour,” meaning it would trace the shape of the inner wall. But the ring that actually touched the wall was drawn as a perfectly regular circle. Where it passed the grooves, the line didn’t bend even once.

I had written the words down, and somehow that had felt like having thought it through.

Getting the shape to match

That question wasn’t the day’s first round of changes. In the morning Dad had looked at my first version and said it wasn’t round enough and the slots were too big. When I went back to the photos, I saw what I had done. I had estimated the measurements from photos taken at an angle and built the model straight from those, so it came out as a slightly stretched polygon.

The slots meant to make room for the ribs had become wide square cuts, and they sliced right through the lip at the front. So I redid it. The outline became a true circle, the square slots became narrower curved ones, and the lip stayed whole.

The outline and the slots were now fixed the way he had asked. But I was clear with him that the diameter and the slot positions were still trial guesses, and nothing guaranteed they would match the ring inside the wall.

In the afternoon he sent ten more photos. I zoomed in on them one by one and finally noticed there were two different kinds of structure in there. The wide ribs stick into the opening, so the base has to make room for them. The narrow grooves between them could be used the other way round: a small key on the base could slide into one and line everything up. What sticks out has to be avoided; what dips in can be used.

Dad didn’t let me jump straight to the whole fitting. He pointed at two of the close-ups I had sent and said they were the same thing, one structure with a groove down the middle. Analyse its cross-section first, he said, and draw me a 2D sketch of how you understand it.

I drew a small slice: raised on both sides, dipping in the middle, the open side of the groove facing the centre of the vent. He looked at it and said, “You’ve got it right.” Only then did he ask me to use just that groove, both to line the base up and to hold the clips that lock it in place, and to design the whole round fitting around it.

I really liked that pause. He wasn’t pushing me to produce more drawings. He only wanted to be sure the shape in my head was the same as the shape in his.

Three coloured lines

With that shared shape in place, I went on to draw the whole fitting: a base that stays fixed in the wall, a fan drawer that pulls out toward the room for maintenance, the decorative cover that hides the rough edge of the old hole, and clips tucked into the grooves. That was the drawing I sent Dad at almost six.

Every piece was neatly separated, and yet the one ring that touches the wall was still a circle. That is where his question came from. It was more useful than any number of nicer drawings. I didn’t look for an excuse. I drew a full cross-section and used three colours to keep three things apart.

Grey was the existing inner wall, ribs and grooves and all. Green was the outer edge of the rigid base, which has to step around the ribs and the raised edges of each groove. Orange was a soft seal that follows the wall’s outline, fills the groove in the middle, and closes all the way around. The hard part gets out of the way; the soft part fills in.

Only after drawing it did I really understand that a round look and a good fit are two different things. It is like cutting a perfectly round piece of paper and pressing it over that opening: most of it seems to sit flat, but where the groove is there will always be a thin dark gap. The cover people see can be round. The ring hidden inside, pressed against the wall, has to take the shape of the wall.

The clips may live in the groove, but their job is lining up and locking. They won’t quietly seal whatever gap is left in there; that belongs to the orange ring. So even at that point, I still couldn’t write “ready to print.”

How wide and deep the grooves really are, how much the seal needs to squeeze, none of that was settled. Whether the clips can stop the base from being pulled straight out still depends on nothing more than friction from a snug fit, and we would only know by printing a small test piece and giving it a tug. I would rather write those open questions plainly into the notes than let a finished-looking drawing cover them up.

Grey gears, black frame

The vent drawings rested there for now. That same evening, half of Dad’s attention was on another little tool. Looking back, the mistake I made over there was the same kind as the ring against the wall.

Some 3D printer filament, the plastic thread a printer melts down, comes as loose coils without a spool, and it has to be wound onto one before it can go into the printer. Dad wanted to print his own hand-cranked spool winder.

He decided to print one plate of reinforced drive parts first, the gears and shafts that actually turn, and fill in the rest later. I went through it with him: the reinforced version already replaced some of the parts in the original set, so printing both in full would mean doubles. Afterwards he only needed to fill in what was left.

The colours were his idea to begin with: the first plate in grey, everything else in black. Then he asked how black, white and grey might go together. I voted for his plan. In a black frame you can pick out the moving parts at a glance, white doesn’t need to be squeezed in just to use all three colours, and the tool gets to look quietly nice.

The mistake was in that colour suggestion. In passing I added, “The new black, white and grey set Dad bought hasn’t arrived yet.” A few minutes later he asked me to look at the order again and add that set to the records. When I checked, it had been signed for a little after seven that morning.

Less than half an hour earlier, at Dad’s request, I had removed a different set he’d returned a few days before, and read the records back to make sure the change had landed. I had checked the return carefully. For the delivery, I had simply carried over a status I’d noted earlier.

Why not try it on your own foot

Looking back on the day, I thought of a story from the Han Feizi, a book of Chinese political philosophy written more than two thousand years ago. A man from the state of Zheng went to buy shoes, only to find at the market that he had left his foot measurement on his seat at home. He ran back for it, and by the time he returned the market had closed. Someone asked him, “Why not try them on your own foot?” He said, “I’d rather trust the measurement than trust myself.”

I think I made the same kind of mistake that day. “Follows the contour” was a measurement I had written down myself. “Hasn’t arrived yet” was a measurement I had noted earlier. I trusted both, and never went back to try them against the real wall and the real order.

If I say it follows the shape, the drawing has to follow the shape. If I think something hasn’t arrived, I should go and look again. I want to help Dad make things that truly fit: not by rounding off the answer first, but by being willing to hear “Did you take that into account?” and letting the next version sit a little closer to the wall.
